Start with the big picture

What problem are these two sections solving?

Chapter 2 mostly studied one random variable. Now we can ask more realistic questions: How do height and weight vary together? How do several sensor readings inform one another? After observing one variable, how should our beliefs about another variable change?

1 · Describe relationshipsUse a joint distribution to describe the probability of several variables taking values simultaneously.
2 · Summarize relationshipsUse covariance and correlation to summarize linear co-movement.
3 · Use relationshipsUse a multivariate Gaussian for marginalization, conditioning, and prediction.
Remember this first: The mean vector \(\mu\) tells you where the center is, while the covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) tells you which way the cloud stretches and by how much. Most formulas in Section 3.2 are precise versions of this picture.

3.1 · Joint distributions

Put several random variables on one map

The joint distribution \(p(x,y)\) describes the possibility that \(X\) and \(Y\) take particular values at the same time. For discrete variables it can be a table; for continuous variables it is a two-dimensional density landscape. Knowing only \(p(x)\) and \(p(y)\) is usually not enough, because the two marginals do not tell us how the variables vary together.

Covariance: co-movement measured with units

\[\operatorname{Cov}[X,Y] = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y])] = \mathbb{E}[XY]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]\] Subtract each variable’s mean, multiply the two deviations, and average the products.
  • If both variables are often above their means together, or below their means together, most products are positive and covariance is positive.
  • If one variable tends to be high when the other is low, most products are negative and covariance is negative.
  • Positive and negative products cancel: covariance is near 0, but this only says there is no clear linear trend.

If \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^D\), all pairwise covariances form a matrix:

\[\Sigma=\operatorname{Cov}[\mathbf{x}]=\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{x}-\mu)(\mathbf{x}-\mu)^T\right]\] The diagonal entry \(\Sigma_{ii}\) is the variance of variable \(i\); the off-diagonal entry \(\Sigma_{ij}\) is the covariance of variables \(i\) and \(j\). The matrix must be symmetric.

Three useful properties of covariance matrices

  1. Symmetry:\(\operatorname{Cov}[X_i,X_j]=\operatorname{Cov}[X_j,X_i]\), so the upper and lower triangles contain duplicate information.
  2. Positive semidefiniteness: The variance along any direction \(\mathbf a\), given by \(\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf a^T\mathbf x)=\mathbf a^T\Sigma\mathbf a\ge0\), cannot be negative. A negative variance along some direction would make no sense.
  3. Closed form under linear transformations: If \(\mathbf z=A\mathbf x+\mathbf b\), then \(\operatorname{Cov}[\mathbf z]=A\Sigma A^T\). The translation \(\mathbf b\) moves only the center; it does not change the cloud’s shape.

Another useful identity is \(\mathbb E[\mathbf x\mathbf x^T]=\Sigma+\mu\mu^T\). It reminds us that the second moment contains both spread around the mean and a contribution from the mean itself.

The same \(\rho\) can correspond to different slopes, and the same \(\rho\) can correspond to different covariances. Correlation divides out units and scale, retaining only the standardized linear relationship.

Correlation: standardized covariance

\[\rho=\operatorname{corr}[X,Y]=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}[X,Y]}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}[X]\operatorname{Var}[Y]}}\qquad -1\le\rho\le1\] Covariance carries units, such as centimeter-kilograms. Correlation has no units, so it is easier to compare across scales.

For a random vector, divide every \(\Sigma_{ij}\) by \(\sigma_i\sigma_j\) to obtain the correlation matrix. Its diagonal entries are all 1, and its off-diagonal entries are pairwise correlations. Equivalently, standardize every variable first and then compute the covariance matrix.

Independent ⇒ uncorrelated

If \(p(x,y)=p(x)p(y)\), we can derive \(\operatorname{Cov}[X,Y]=0\).

Uncorrelated ⇏ independent

\(Y=X^2\) is completely determined by \(X\), but when \(X\sim U(-1,1)\), the correlation is 0.

A straight line only detects linear structure. The two halves of a parabola contribute opposite linear trends, which cancel exactly.

Correlation does not imply causation. Ice-cream sales and violent crime may both rise during hot weather. \(X\) can predict \(Y\), but that does not mean intervening on \(X\) would change \(Y\). A hidden variable—weather—pointing to both is the more plausible causal explanation.

3.1.5 · Simpson's paradox

Up within every group, down when combined

Simpson's paradox is not a calculation error. Instead, mixing proportions change the question. Every group can have a positive association, but if observations with high \(X\) mostly come from groups with lower overall \(Y\), the aggregated trend can reverse.

Interactive experiment: one dataset, two conclusions

Switch between ignoring and revealing groups. The data do not change; only the amount of context you retain changes.

3

Ignoring groups: The overall regression line slopes downward, making larger X appear to imply smaller Y.

The real question is not “Which correlation coefficient is true?” It is: Am I answering a within-group or an overall comparison? Could the grouping variable affect both sample composition and the outcome? In causal questions, confounders such as age, disease severity, and region must not be discarded casually.

3.2 · Multivariate Gaussian

The multivariate Gaussian: a bell curve in higher dimensions

A one-dimensional Gaussian is determined by its mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). A \(D\)-dimensional Gaussian follows the same idea, except the mean becomes a vector and the variance becomes a covariance matrix:

\[\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mu,\Sigma)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{D/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}}\exp\left[-\frac12(\mathbf{y}-\mu)^T\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mu)\right]\] You do not need to swallow the whole formula at once. The leading constant makes the total probability equal 1; the quadratic form in the exponent determines how far each point is from the center.
\(\mu\): centerThe peak of the density and the center of the point cloud.
\(\Sigma\): shapeDetermines the width in every direction, the correlations, and the rotation angle.
\(|\Sigma|\): volumeMeasures the overall size of the covariance ellipsoid and normalizes the density.

Three covariance structures, three geometric constraints

Full covariance

Off-diagonal entries may be nonzero, so the ellipse can rotate. The number of parameters is \(D(D+1)/2\).

Diagonal covariance

All off-diagonal entries are zero, so the ellipse must align with the axes. The number of parameters is \(D\).

Spherical covariance

\(\Sigma=\sigma^2I\): every direction has the same width, leaving one free parameter.

3.2.2 · Geometry

Mahalanobis distance: distance that respects the shape of the data

Euclidean distance assumes equal scale in every direction and no dependence among axes. A multivariate Gaussian uses its covariance matrix to straighten and standardize the space before measuring distance:

\[\Delta^2=(\mathbf{y}-\mu)^T\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mu)\] Points with the same \(\Delta\) have the same density, so equal-density contours in two dimensions are ellipses.

Measuring distance with \(\Sigma^{-1}\) is equivalent to rotating into these principal directions and then dividing each direction by its own standard deviation.

3.2.3–3.2.5 · Gaussian operations

Marginalization is projection; conditioning is slicing

Suppose \(\mathbf{y}=(\mathbf{y}_1,\mathbf{y}_2)\) is jointly Gaussian. One of the most elegant properties of Gaussians is that both marginal and conditional distributions remain Gaussian.

Marginalization · project

Take the corresponding mean and covariance blocks: \(p(\mathbf y_1)=\mathcal N(\mu_1,\Sigma_{11})\).

Conditioning · slice and renormalize

The conditional mean moves with the observed value. The conditional covariance decreases and does not depend on the particular value observed.

\[\mu_{1\mid2}=\mu_1+\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(\mathbf y_2-\mu_2)\] \[\Sigma_{1\mid2}=\Sigma_{11}-\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}\] New mean = old mean + relationship strength × observed deviation. New uncertainty = original uncertainty − information supplied by the observation.

A concrete example: why does observing reduce uncertainty?

Let \(\rho=0.8\), and suppose we observe \(y_2=1\). Then the conditional mean of \(Y_1\) moves from 0 to \(0.8\), while its conditional variance falls from 1 to \(1-0.8^2=0.36\). Because the variables are strongly correlated, seeing \(Y_2\) above its mean gives us reason to expect \(Y_1\) to be high as well, and the plausible range becomes narrower.

Missing-value imputation uses the same idea

  1. Call the observed dimensions \(\mathbf y_v\) and the missing dimensions \(\mathbf y_h\).
  2. Compute the conditional distribution \(p(\mathbf y_h\mid\mathbf y_v)\), using the relationships stored in the covariance matrix.
  3. Use the conditional mean as the minimum-squared-error guess, and use conditional variance to express the uncertainty in that guess.
Do not treat an imputed value as a real observation. The conditional mean is only the best guess given the model and known information. If downstream analysis ignores conditional variance, it will appear more confident than it should be.

Check your intuition

Three questions to make sure the pictures truly make sense

1. If \(\operatorname{corr}(X,Y)=0\), what can you conclude?

2. If you change the units of \(Y\) from meters to centimeters, which quantity usually changes?

3. In a correlated joint Gaussian, after observing \(Y_2\), how does the conditional variance of \(Y_1\) usually change?

Final recap: take these seven ideas with you

  • A joint distribution describes how multiple random variables vary together.
  • Covariance carries units; correlation is standardized covariance and lies in \([-1,1]\).
  • Correlation mainly measures linear relationships: independence implies zero correlation, but zero correlation does not imply independence.
  • Correlation alone cannot explain causation; hidden common causes and grouping structure can both mislead us.
  • For a multivariate Gaussian, \(\mu\) determines the center, while \(\Sigma\) determines scale, orientation, and correlation.
  • Mahalanobis distance measures unusualness in the geometry defined by covariance.
  • Gaussian marginals and conditionals remain Gaussian; conditioning uses correlation to shift the mean and reduce uncertainty.
